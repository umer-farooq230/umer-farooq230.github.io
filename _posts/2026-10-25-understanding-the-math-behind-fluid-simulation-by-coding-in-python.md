<figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*5fefzHuAa5Ty50a-sX_fnA.jpeg" /><figcaption>Source: code execution screenshot</figcaption></figure><p>I’ve always thought of optimization as an abstract technique. For me, whenever the backend was laggy or responding late, I figured it was because of sequential table scans in the DB or bad memory allocation, and I was able to solve them with some existing techniques.</p><p>Still, the idea of “optimization” stuck with me and I couldn’t grasp it properly. What does it mean to optimize a system?</p><h3>The Bigger Idea</h3><p>That was until I made a fluid simulation in Python. Here’s the high-level idea:</p><ol><li>Make the particles. Each particle is considered a drop.</li><li>The liquid particles have to stay close, but not too close (they would become a solid), and far, but not too far (they would become a gas).</li><li>When a liquid particle is disturbed from one place to another, the close ones rush in to fill that space immediately.</li></ol><p>Even though this is the intuitive idea, there are deeper things here.</p><p>To simulate properly, we need the following calculations:</p><ul><li>Density</li><li>Pressure</li><li>Pressure Force</li><li>Velocity</li><li>Position</li></ul><h3>Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH)</h3><p>Now we bring in the science behind our points above. In the SPH paper, the concept of a smoothing radius was introduced, which defines how far a particle can influence other particles when calculating properties such as density, pressure and velocity.</p><p>For two particles Xi and Xj, with a smoothing radius h = 2.0, we calculate the distance r = |Xi − Xj|. If r &lt; h, the neighboring particle contributes to the calculation. Otherwise, it doesn’t.</p><p>The whole point of the smoothing kernel is to assign more weight to the nearby particles and less to the distant ones.</p><h3>Density</h3><p>Think of density as how crowded the particles are around a particular particle.</p><p>If you have ten particles packed into a small space, the density will be higher than if those same ten particles are spread across a larger space. But how do we calculate this for a single particle when there are no fixed boxes or containers around it?</p><p>This is where the smoothing kernel comes in.</p><p>For a particle i, we calculate its density by looking at its neighboring particles, considering their mass and how far away they are.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/256/1*V2fxD7FMShX2t8Lm9RCXBg.png" /><figcaption>source: Latex converted image</figcaption></figure><p>Here, mⱼ is the mass of a neighboring particle, and W is the smoothing kernel we discussed above.</p><p>The equation basically says: take each neighboring particle’s mass, multiply it by its influence, and add everything together.</p><p>A nearby particle contributes more, while a distant particle contributes less. If there are more particles crowded around our particle, the calculated density increases.</p><p>The interesting part is that we are estimating density without dividing a container into fixed regions. Each particle calculates its density from its surroundings.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/720/1*7e8m7p_ME-A5xaAA_Of-XA.jpeg" /><figcaption>Source: <a href="https://www.slideserve.com/gloria/igert-sph-and-free-surface-flows">IGERT: SPH and Free Surface Flows</a></figcaption></figure><h3>Pressure</h3><p>Now that we have density, we can start understanding pressure.</p><p>Imagine pushing your hand into a bucket of water. The water resists being compressed. In a fluid simulation, we need a way to represent this behavior mathematically.</p><p>One common approach is to compare the current density with a target density, also called the rest density.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/149/1*pw88MhP5ShL1qX7pQsUBIg.png" /><figcaption>source: Latex converted image</figcaption></figure><p>Here, ρᵢ is the density we just calculated, ρ₀ is the target density, and k controls how strongly pressure responds to changes in density.</p><p>If the particles become too crowded, their density rises above the target, and pressure increases. This pressure helps push them apart.</p><p>Think of the target density as the crowding level we want the fluid to maintain. The simulation uses pressure to respond when particles deviate from that level.</p><p>Of course, this is a simplified pressure equation. More advanced simulations can use different equations of state, but the intuition remains similar.</p><h3>Pressure Force</h3><p>We now know how crowded the particles are and how much pressure they have. But how does this actually make them move?</p><p>Let’s imagine two regions of water. One is crowded, and the other has more room. The difference in pressure creates a force that tends to push the fluid away from the higher-pressure region.</p><p>This is where the pressure gradient comes in.</p><p>A gradient tells us how quickly something changes across space and in which direction it increases. In our case, it tells us how pressure changes around a particle.</p><p>The pressure force acts in the opposite direction to the pressure gradient, pushing fluid from higher-pressure regions toward lower-pressure regions.</p><p>In SPH, we calculate this using the pressures and densities of neighboring particles, along with the gradient of the smoothing kernel.</p><p>We don’t need to memorize the full equation to understand what is happening. Each neighbor contributes to the force on our particle, and the simulation adds those contributions together.</p><p>The important thing is that pressure is not just a number attached to a particle. Differences in pressure between neighboring particles are what help determine how the fluid moves.</p><h3>Velocity</h3><p>Now we have forces acting on our particles. What happens next?</p><p>Newton’s second law tells us that force produces acceleration:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/82/1*DjENI1Nf4j1rUR6D7--_eQ.png" /><figcaption>source: Latex converted image</figcaption></figure><p>So, once we calculate the forces acting on a particle, we can calculate its acceleration.</p><p>But acceleration and velocity are not the same thing. Acceleration changes velocity over time.</p><p>For a small time step, we can update velocity using:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/176/1*hHZCXuvF-quWEGz8-ANxCw.png" /><figcaption>source: Latex converted image</figcaption></figure><p>Here, Δt is the small interval of time for which we are simulating the motion.</p><p>For example, if a particle is moving at 2 m/s and experiences an acceleration of 3 m/s² for 0.1 seconds, its velocity becomes 2.3 m/s.</p><p>The same idea applies to every particle in the simulation. Pressure, gravity, and other forces change their velocities over time.</p><h3>Position</h3><p>Finally, we use the velocity to update the position of each particle.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/180/1*n_PoRfsD-7o8-JDmnNiZTw.png" /><figcaption>source: Latex converted image</figcaption></figure><p>If a particle is moving at 2.3 m/s for 0.1 seconds, it moves approximately 0.23 meters during that time step.</p><p>This gives us the particle’s new position.</p><p>But remember, moving one particle changes its distance from the others. That means the smoothing kernel weights can change, which affects density, pressure, and the forces calculated in the next step.</p><p>And this is where everything connects.</p><h3>Bringing It All Together</h3><p>We started with a collection of particles and wanted to make them behave like a fluid.</p><p>To do that, we estimate how crowded each particle’s surroundings are, calculate pressure from density, and use pressure differences to determine forces. Those forces change velocity, and velocity changes position.</p><p>Then we repeat the process.</p><p>Position → Density → Pressure → Force → Velocity → Position</p><p>Each iteration changes the state of the simulation, and each new state influences the next one.</p><p>This is the part I find interesting. What looks like a fluid moving naturally on the screen is actually the result of repeatedly calculating local interactions between particles.</p><p>We aren’t explicitly telling the water how to flow around every obstacle or how to fill every gap. Instead, we define the rules governing the particles and let their interactions produce the motion.</p><p>There is still a lot more to fluid simulation, including viscosity, boundary conditions, and numerical stability. But understanding these basic calculations gives us a foundation for exploring them.</p><p>And for me, this is where the abstract idea of simulation starts to make sense: a complex system can emerge from a relatively small set of rules, applied repeatedly.</p><h3>Making in Python</h3><p>The theory above is clean. The code is where it gets messy, so here are the functions that actually matter, and what each one does to the fluid you see on the screen.</p><p>One honest note first. The code is not the loop from the sections above, line by line. The classic version calculates a pressure force, turns it into velocity, and then moves the particle. My first version did exactly that and ran at around 10 FPS. The problem is that pressure forces are touchy. If the time step is too big, particles overshoot, push each other even harder, and the whole thing explodes. So you are forced to take tiny steps, which means a lot of calculations for every frame.</p><p>The version I ended up with is called Position Based Fluids. It skips the force. It predicts where every particle is about to land, moves the crowded ones apart until the density looks right, and then works out the velocity from how far each one moved. Same idea, different order. Think of it as walking someone to the right seat instead of shoving them and hoping they land there.</p><h3>The smoothing kernel and density</h3><pre>def smoothing_kernel(radius, distance):<br>    volume = math.pi * radius ** 8 / 4.0<br>    value = np.maximum(0.0, radius * radius - distance * distance)<br>    return value ** 3 / volume</pre><p>This is the “more weight to nearby particles, less to distant ones” idea as code. The middle line is the clever one. radius² − distance² is large when a neighbor is close and shrinks to zero right at the smoothing radius, and np.maximum(0, ...) makes sure anything beyond it gets exactly zero influence. Cubing it makes the weight fade out smoothly instead of switching off like a light, which keeps the fluid from twitching every time a particle crosses the edge of someone&#39;s neighborhood. Dividing by volume keeps the total weight consistent, so changing the radius doesn&#39;t change the density on its own.</p><p>The radius is a real trade-off. Make it too small and particles barely see each other, so the fluid turns into loose sand. Make it too big and everything blurs together, and the simulation slows down because every particle has more neighbors to check.</p><pre>def calculate_density(positions, sample_point, smoothing_radius, particle_mass):<br>    distances = np.linalg.norm(positions - sample_point, axis=1)<br>    return float(np.sum(particle_mass * smoothing_kernel(smoothing_radius, distances)))</pre><p>This is the density equation from earlier, almost word for word. The first line measures how far every neighbor is. The second weights each one with the kernel, multiplies by mass, and adds it all up. The result is a crowdedness meter, and everything else in the simulation reacts to this one number. In my app it powers the “density under the cursor” readout. The solver itself does the same sum inline, inside a function we will meet in a moment.</p><h3>The line above every function</h3><pre>@njit(parallel=True, cache=True, fastmath=True)<br>def solve_lambda(...):<br>    for particle in prange(count):</pre><p>Normal Python reads a loop one instruction at a time, which is painfully slow when the loop runs over thousands of particles, several times per frame. @njit (from a library called Numba) translates the function into machine code the first time it runs, which is why the first launch takes a few seconds. prange goes one step further. Each particle&#39;s calculation doesn&#39;t depend on the others, so the work is split across every CPU core at the same time.</p><p>No formula changed. The fluid behaves exactly the same, it just gets there much sooner. It is the cheapest optimization in the whole project: one line, and no change to the maths.</p><h3>Finding neighbors without checking everyone</h3><p>Everything above needs one thing, a particle’s neighbors. The naive way is to measure the distance from every particle to every other particle. With 9,000 particles, that is about 81 million checks per pass, and we do several passes per frame.</p><pre>cell_x = min(max(int(pred[particle, 0] * inv_cell_size), 0), grid_cols - 1)<br>cell_y = min(max(int(pred[particle, 1] * inv_cell_size), 0), grid_rows - 1)<br>cell = cell_y * grid_cols + cell_x</pre><p>Think of cutting the screen into squares, each exactly as wide as the smoothing radius. These three lines just work out which square a particle is standing in. Then comes the useful part:</p><pre>for cell_row in range(max(cell_y - 1, 0), min(cell_y + 2, grid_rows)):<br>    for cell_col in range(max(cell_x - 1, 0), min(cell_x + 2, grid_cols)):<br>        cell = cell_row * grid_cols + cell_col<br>        for slot in range(cell_start[cell], cell_start[cell + 1]):<br>            neighbor = order[slot]</pre><p>Anyone further away than the smoothing radius has zero influence anyway, so a particle’s neighbors can only be in its own square or the eight around it. Instead of checking thousands of particles, each one checks a few dozen. The answer is identical and the fluid looks the same. The work just dropped enormously.</p><p>If this sounds familiar, it is the same idea as a database index. A sequential table scan reads every row to find the few that matter. The grid jumps straight to them.</p><h3>Gravity and the prediction</h3><pre>vel_y = vel[particle, 1] + gravity * dt<br>...<br>pred[particle, 0] = pos_x + vel_x * dt<br>pred[particle, 1] = pos_y + vel_y * dt</pre><p>The first line is gravity. Every step, every particle’s downward velocity goes up a little. Take it out and the fluid floats like it is in space. Make it bigger and the same liquid feels heavy.</p><p>The last two lines are the prediction, and they are the part I find most interesting. We don’t move the particle yet. We write down where it would end up if nothing else interfered: position + velocity × time step. Think of it as a rough draft of the next frame. The rest of the calculation edits this draft, and only then do we make it real. Because the density is measured on the draft instead of the old positions, crowding gets fixed before it ever shows up on the screen, rather than after a crowd has already formed.</p><p>The same function also handles the mouse, where grabbed particles are steered toward the cursor, and a speed limit at the end stops one violent frame from launching particles across the screen.</p><h3>How crowded, and how hard to push</h3><pre>density_error = density * inv_rest_density - 1.0      # &gt; 0 means crowded<br>if density_error &gt; 0.0:<br>    lagrange[particle] = -density_error / (<br>        neighbor_grad_sq_sum + grad_sum_x * grad_sum_x<br>        + grad_sum_y * grad_sum_y + softening)<br>else:<br>    lagrange[particle] = 0.0</pre><p>This is the pressure idea from earlier wearing a different outfit. density_error compares a particle&#39;s density with the rest density. A value of 0.2 means the particle is 20% too crowded.</p><p>The if is a deliberate choice. A particle that isn&#39;t crowded gets no push at all, so we only resist squashing, never stretching. If we also pulled stretched fluid back together, the fluid would clump into blobs. Leaving it out lets a splash scatter into droplets.</p><p>The number we store is how hard this particle must be pushed to fix its crowding. The more crowded it is, the bigger the push, and the negative sign means away. The long bottom part scales the push by how sensitive the density is to movement, so a particle squeezed in a tight spot isn’t over-corrected. And softening is a small safety cushion so this division can never blow up and fire a particle across the screen.</p><h3>Pushing the particles apart</h3><pre>overlap = smoothing_radius - distance<br>grad_scale = spiky * overlap * overlap * mass * inv_rest_density / distance<br>pair_lagrange = own_lagrange + lagrange[neighbor]<br>move_x += pair_lagrange * grad_scale * offset_x<br>move_y += pair_lagrange * grad_scale * offset_y</pre><p>This is where the pressure gradient from the earlier section finally shows up. grad_scale is the gradient of the smoothing kernel. It decides how hard two particles shove each other: a lot when they are almost touching, fading to nothing at the edge of the smoothing radius. Multiplying by offset, the direction from the neighbor to me, turns that into a push along the line between the two. Add up the push from every neighbor and each particle ends up with one move.</p><p>Using own_lagrange + lagrange[neighbor] keeps the push fair. Particle A moves away from B by exactly as much as B moves away from A, a bit like Newton&#39;s third law, so a crowd spreads out evenly instead of drifting to one side.</p><pre>if move_length &gt; max_correction:<br>    move_x *= max_correction / move_length<br>    move_y *= max_correction / move_length</pre><p>This cap says no particle may move more than a fraction of the smoothing radius in one pass. Without it, two particles that start almost on top of each other would catapult one another across the screen.</p><h3>Turning movement into velocity</h3><pre>vel[particle, 0] = (pred[particle, 0] - pos[particle, 0]) * inv_dt<br>vel[particle, 1] = (pred[particle, 1] - pos[particle, 1]) * inv_dt</pre><p>Velocity is distance divided by time. After all that pushing, the draft positions have moved away from where the particles actually were, and these two lines measure how far, divided by the time step. This is how pressure turns into motion. A particle pushed upward by a crowd now carries upward velocity into the next frame, and that carried-over momentum is what makes a splash. Without these lines, the pushes would only shuffle particles around, and none of that motion would continue into the next frame.</p><h3>Back to optimization</h3><p>So, what does it mean to optimize a system?</p><p>Looking back, none of the optimizations changed what the simulation was trying to do. The grid didn’t change a single density value, it just stopped me from checking particles that could never matter. Numba didn’t change a formula, it changed how fast, and on how many cores, the formulas run. Even the firmness dial is a decision about how much work is worth doing.</p><p>For me, that is the answer. Optimizing a system isn’t about making everything faster. It is about finding the work that never needed to happen, and then the work that can happen at the same time. The grid is basically a database index, and the parallel loop is the same idea as spreading requests across workers. I had met these ideas on the backend before, but I never saw them this clearly until I could watch the frame rate change.</p><p>This article was inspired by <a href="https://www.youtube.com/c/SebastianLague">Sebastian Lague’s Youtube channel</a> and his video on <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rSKMYc1CQHE&amp;list=PLFt_AvWsXl0dJrPelwdVSMVC_MDTYPJWn">Coding Adventure Simulating Fluids</a></p><p>Follow on <a href="http://www.linkedin.com/in/umer-farooq230">Linkedin</a> and <a href="https://www.instagram.com/gradientindecent">Instagram</a></p><img src="https://medium.com/_/stat?event=post.clientViewed&referrerSource=full_rss&postId=a21c530dfcf0" width="1" height="1" alt=""><hr><p><a href="https://towardsdev.com/understanding-the-math-behind-fluid-simulation-by-coding-in-python-a21c530dfcf0">Understanding the Math Behind Fluid Simulation by Coding in Python</a> was originally published in <a href="https://towardsdev.com">Towards Dev</a> on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.</p>
